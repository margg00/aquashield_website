## Two questions, two tools

A smart water meter answers one question: how much water did this unit use? That is a billing question, and meters answer it well. Leak detection answers a different one: is water going somewhere it should not? That question is about the network, not the unit.

The confusion is understandable. Both involve water, sensors and a dashboard. But a meter sits at the end of the pipe, where water is delivered. A leak detection sensor sits on the riser or the supply line, where water is shared. Hidden leaks happen in the shared part.

## Why a leak hides from meters

Picture a riser feeding ten flats. A joint on the riser between floors 3 and 4 starts weeping. The water runs down inside the shaft. It passes no meter, because it never reaches a flat. The building's main meter sees a small increase, but the main meter sees everything, and everything swings by far more from day to day than a weeping joint adds. The leak is inside the noise.

This is the pattern behind most of the damage that German insurers paid EUR 4.91bn for in 2024: not a burst pipe that floods a flat in an hour, but a slow leak that ran for weeks before anyone saw a stain.

## What leak detection does differently

AquaShield's sensors clamp onto the riser and the supply lines, in under 15 minutes each, with no pipe cutting. They read flow every 10 minutes. A model per measuring point learns what normal looks like for that riser: the night baseline, the morning peak, the weekly rhythm. A weeping joint changes the night baseline, because water keeps moving when every tap is closed. The model sees the pattern that no meter can.

Then the digital twin places it. Because the twin knows which sensor sees which water, a deviation on the riser with no deviation on the branches points at the riser itself. The alert names the riser and the floors to check.

## Keep the meters, add the view

Nothing about this replaces the meters. Billing stays where it is. What a portfolio adds is a live view of the part of the network that meters never see, and a model that knows the difference between a Tuesday morning and a leak.

## Three questions to ask your meter provider

If a portfolio is deciding between adding meters and adding detection, three questions make the difference clear.

1. **Does it read the risers, or the units?** Meters read units. A leak in the shared network sits upstream of every unit.
2. **What is the reading interval?** Monthly or daily totals cannot show a night baseline. Detection reads every 10 minutes, because the difference between normal and leak is clearest at 3 a.m.
3. **Does it learn?** A meter reports consumption. Detection learns each riser's normal and ranks deviations by severity, so the team gets a place to check rather than a number to interpret.

The answers usually show that the two tools are not competing. One bills, the other watches.
